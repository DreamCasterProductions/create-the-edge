MODULE J · FOUNDATIONS
J.0 · Why This Section Exists

Why the Debrief Decides the Next Win

The work does not end when the meeting ends or the event closes. It ends when the team has named what worked, what did not, and what to change. A firm that skips the debrief repeats its mistakes at full price.

Every presentation, every proposal review, every event [Company Name] runs produces two outputs: the result, and the lesson. Most firms keep the result and lose the lesson. The deliberate evaluation is what captures the second one. It is a short, honest conversation, structured so that praise and criticism both land on something specific, and it converts the experience of one meeting into judgment the whole team carries into the next.

❞ Expert Insight
"The teams that improve are the ones that sit down afterward and say out loud what went well and what did not. The ones that just move on to the next thing make the same mistake three years running and never notice."
[Training Program] · The Proposal Standard
Figure J.0.1 · What an Honest Evaluation Produces
1
Produces
A Named Lesson
The vague sense that something went wrong becomes a specific, stated cause that the team can actually act on next time.
2
Produces
A Repeatable Win
What worked is identified and made deliberate, so a one-time success becomes a standard the team can reproduce.
3
Produces
Shared Judgment
The lesson lives in the team, not in one person's head, so the next person facing the same situation already knows better.
4
Produces
An Action
Every evaluation ends in a change to make or a thing to keep, assigned to someone, not a discussion that evaporates.
▦ Rule of the House
No meeting or event is closed until it is evaluated. The debrief is part of the work, not an optional extra after it. The evaluation that gets skipped because everyone was busy is the one whose lesson gets paid for again at the next event.
MODULE J · FOUNDATIONS
J.1 · The Framework

What a SWOT Analysis Is

SWOT stands for Strengths, Weaknesses, Opportunities, and Threats. It is a simple grid that forces a balanced look: two quadrants on what you control now, two on what the future holds.
Figure J.1.1 · The Four Quadrants
Strengths
Internal · Present
What are we doing well that we should keep and build on? What the team or firm is doing well right now, within its power to continue.
Weaknesses
Internal · Present
What are we doing poorly that is within our power to fix? Internal gaps, bottlenecks, and breakdowns the team owns and can address.
Opportunities
External · Future
What outside change could we turn to our advantage? Openings in the market, client, or situation that the team could convert into a gain.
Threats
External · Future
What outside risk could hurt us if we do not prepare? External risks outside the team's direct control that could hurt the next effort.
✓ Best Practice: Remember the Two Axes
Internal versus external, and present versus future. Strengths and weaknesses are about you, right now, and you can change them directly. Opportunities and threats are about the world around you, and you respond to them rather than control them. Keeping the axes straight stops the grid from collapsing into one vague list.
☰ From the Files
SWOT is not only a planning tool for the future; it is equally an evaluation tool for the past. After an event, the same four quadrants ask what went well, what went badly, what the result opens up next, and what could undermine the next one. [Company Name] uses it both ways.
MODULE J · FOUR QUADRANTS
J.2 · Strengths

Strengths: What Is Working

Strengths are the things the team did well and should keep doing. Naming them matters as much as naming problems, because a win nobody identifies is a win nobody repeats on purpose.

Strengths are internal and present: the capabilities, habits, and decisions that are working in the team's favor right now. It is tempting to rush past them to get to the problems, but that is a mistake. A strength that is named becomes a standard the team repeats deliberately. A strength left unnamed was luck, and luck does not show up reliably. The discipline is to state what worked specifically enough that someone could do it again.

Figure J.2.1 · Finding Real Strengths
AskWhy It Matters
What went better than expected?Surfaces the wins that would otherwise be taken for granted
What did we do that we should repeat?Turns a one-time success into a deliberate standard
What advantage do we have that competitors do not?Identifies what to lean on harder in the next effort
Who or what made the difference?Credits the specific cause so it can be resourced again
❞ Expert Insight
"When something goes really well, stop and figure out exactly why before you move on. That is the thing you want to bottle and do every single time. Most people only analyze the failures and let the wins slip past unexamined."
[Training Program] · Sales Intelligence Series
✓ Best Practice
Be specific about strengths, not generic. "The early sign-up push worked" is useful because it names a tactic to repeat. "Everyone did great" is not, because no one can act on it. A strength is only worth listing if it is concrete enough to reproduce on purpose.
MODULE J · FOUR QUADRANTS
J.3 · Weaknesses

Weaknesses: What Is Holding Back

Weaknesses are the internal problems the team can actually fix. The honest naming of them, without blame, is what separates a useful evaluation from a comfortable one.

Weaknesses are internal and present: the gaps, bottlenecks, and breakdowns that are within the team's power to address. The hard part is honesty. An evaluation that protects feelings by softening the weaknesses is worthless, because the unspoken problem repeats. The skill is to name the weakness as a problem with a process, not a person, so the conversation stays about fixing the system rather than assigning fault.

Figure J.3.1 · Naming Weaknesses Usefully
Weak FramingUseful Framing
"Check-in was a mess""One line formed instead of two, so people waited and got frustrated"
"Volunteers were useless""Volunteers arrived without a specific assigned task, so they waited to be told what to do"
"We ran short""We capped at 144 but registration crept to 156, and we ran short on golf carts"
"Communication was bad""The task list was emailed but never confirmed, so no one owned the morning roles"
▦ Rule of the House
Name the weakness as a problem with a process, not a verdict on a person. "The hand-off had no owner" can be fixed; "she dropped the ball" starts an argument and fixes nothing. The evaluation exists to improve the system, and that only works when people can be honest without being blamed.
⚠ Common Failure
The polite evaluation that lists only strengths because no one wants to criticize. It feels good and changes nothing. If the debrief produces no weaknesses, it was not honest, and the same problems will surface again at the next event, having cost real money the second time.
MODULE J · FOUR QUADRANTS
J.4 · Opportunities

Opportunities: What Could Be Gained

Opportunities are external openings the team could turn to its advantage. The skill is seeing them, because an opportunity ignored looks exactly like nothing happening at all.

Opportunities are external and forward looking: changes in the market, the client, or the situation that the team could convert into a gain. They are easy to miss because they arrive disguised as ordinary events. A client saying the fee is a little high is, framed correctly, an opportunity to adjust and win the work rather than a rejection. The discipline is to ask, of every development, what advantage it could be turned into.

Figure J.4.1 · Where Opportunities Hide
Looks LikeCould Be the Opportunity To
A client says the fee is a little highAdjust the scope or fee and help them say yes, winning the project
An event drew a strong turnoutAdd sponsorships, videos, or content that builds on the momentum
A presentation went well with one architectBuild the relationship into referrals and repeat work
A new channel is gaining attentionCouple it with existing efforts so each one amplifies the others
❞ Expert Insight
"When a client tells you the proposal is a little too high, that is not a no. That is an opening. It gives you the chance to say, let us adjust this so we can help you win the project. Framed right, the objection is the opportunity."
[Training Program] · 40 Years in the Room
✓ Best Practice
Couple opportunities together rather than chasing them one at a time. An interview becomes a video, the video goes on the channel, the channel feeds the social posts, and each piece amplifies the others. A single opportunity used in isolation is a fraction of what the same effort produces when the pieces are linked.
MODULE J · FOUR QUADRANTS
J.5 · Threats

Threats: What Could Go Wrong

Threats are external risks that could undermine the next effort. Naming them in advance is what turns a threat from a surprise that derails the day into a contingency the team already planned for.

Threats are external and forward looking: the risks outside the team's direct control that could hurt the next project or event. Many threats are predictable from past experience, and naming them is the first step to neutralizing them. Most threats are not disasters waiting to happen; they are simply things that have not been confirmed yet.

Figure J.5.1 · Common Threats and Their Defenses
ThreatThe Defense Naming It Enables
People who never replied but show up on the dayA clear registration cutoff and a plan for walk-ins
Registration exceeding the planned capA firm cap with a known margin, decided before it is hit
Last-minute logistics falling apartAn assigned owner for rides, signage, and coordination
A key dependency arriving late or not at allA confirmation step rather than an assumption it is handled
✓ Best Practice
Treat last year's chaos as this year's threat list. The problems that blindsided the team last time are the most predictable threats this time, because they already happened once. An evaluation that captured them becomes the checklist that prevents them, which is the entire return on doing the debrief.
☰ From the Files
A threat is not always a disaster; sometimes it is simply the thing that has not been confirmed yet. The registrants who have not replied, the contract not yet signed, the volunteer who has not been given a task: each is a threat only until someone closes it. Most threats are neutralized by confirmation, not heroics.
MODULE J · RUNNING THE EVALUATION
J.6 · The Meeting

Running the Meeting Evaluation

The evaluation is a meeting with a shape. Someone leads it, the four quadrants give it structure, and it ends in writing with actions assigned. Without that shape it becomes a venting session that changes nothing.
Figure J.6.1 · The Shape of the Evaluation
1
Step One
Lead It
One person runs the debrief and walks the team through all four quadrants in order, keeping it from drifting into only complaints.
2
Step Two
Hear Everyone
The person who saw the problem is often not the one who owns it. Every role in the room sees a different part of the picture.
3
Step Three
Write It Down
Capture the four quadrants in writing. An evaluation no one recorded is forgotten before the next event needs it.
4
Step Four
Assign Actions
Each change to make gets an owner and a name attached. Insight with no owner is a conversation, not an improvement.
❞ Expert Insight
"What worked last time was giving each volunteer a specific task and having them sign their name to it. Active, named, accountable. The year we just asked for volunteers in general, everyone showed up and stood around asking what to do."
[Training Program] · Sales Intelligence Series
▦ Rule of the House
An evaluation ends with assigned actions or it did not happen. The test of a debrief is not how good the discussion felt but how many specific changes left the room with a name attached. No owner, no action; no action, no improvement.
MODULE J · RUNNING THE EVALUATION
J.7 · Worked Example

The Golf Tournament SWOT: A Real Example

This is a SWOT [Company Name] actually ran. In planning the annual golf tournament, the team built a full analysis of the prior year to shape the next. It shows the framework working on a real event, not a hypothetical.

Each year [Company Name] hosts a golf tournament, and the team plans the next one by analyzing the last. The tournament runs on a known date with an 8am check-in and a 9:30 shotgun start, capped near 144 players. The analysis below is drawn from that real session.

Figure J.7.1 · The Golf Tournament SWOT
QuadrantWhat the Team Identified
StrengthsThe early sign-up push worked well. Raffle prizes were chosen during play and matched to winners on the spot, replacing the old hours-long draw. Two course plans were ready, returning teams came back, and the giveaways were popular.
WeaknessesGolf cart capacity ran short when registration crept from the 144 cap to 156. Check-in bottlenecked into one long line instead of a split by last name. Volunteers arrived without specific assigned tasks and waited to be told what to do.
OpportunitiesRaise the price per player on the new contract. Add three or four videos to promote the event. Give each volunteer a specific named task so they participate actively. Build sponsorships and expand the raffle.
ThreatsPeople who never replied but show up on the day. Registration exceeding the cart capacity again. Last-minute ride and logistics confusion pulling the lead away from running the event.
☰ From the Files
Notice how each quadrant points to an action. The check-in bottleneck (weakness) becomes a plan to split the line and assign a named greeter (action). The no-shows (threat) become a registration cutoff (defense). This is the whole purpose of the grid: every box should generate something the team will do differently next time.
✓ Best Practice
Run the SWOT on last year's event while planning this year's. The team that analyzes the previous tournament before the next one walks in already knowing what to repeat and what to fix. The analysis is most valuable when it sits at the front of planning, not filed away after the fact.
MODULE J · RUNNING THE EVALUATION
J.8 · From Analysis to Action

Turning SWOT into Action

A SWOT grid is not the deliverable. The actions it produces are. Each quadrant pairs with a move: keep the strengths, fix the weaknesses, pursue the opportunities, defend against the threats.
Figure J.8.1 · Each Quadrant Becomes a Move
QuadrantThe Action It Demands
StrengthsMake it deliberate. Write the win into the plan so it is repeated on purpose, not by luck.
WeaknessesAssign a fix. Give the process gap an owner and a specific change before the next effort.
OpportunitiesPursue with an owner. An opportunity nobody is assigned to chase is one nobody chases.
ThreatsPlan a defense. Decide the cutoff, the margin, or the contingency now, while it is calm.
▦ Rule of the House
Every quadrant must produce at least one action with a name on it. A strength with no plan to repeat it, or a threat with no defense assigned, is an observation, not a result. The grid is judged by the actions that leave it, not by how complete the boxes look.
⚠ Common Failure
Treating the SWOT as the finished product. The team fills in four neat boxes, feels productive, and files it. Next event, the same weaknesses recur because the boxes were never converted into assigned changes. The analysis without the action is theater.
MODULE J · CLOSING THE LOOP
J.9 · Follow-Up

Following Up After the Meeting

The evaluation names the actions. The follow-up makes sure they happen. Without it, the best debrief in the world produces a list that everyone agreed with and no one executed.
Figure J.9.1 · The Follow-Up Discipline
StepWhat It Ensures
Confirm each owner accepts the actionNo action is orphaned because the owner never knew it was theirs
Set a date for each actionAn action without a deadline competes with everything urgent and loses
Check progress before the next eventProblems surface while there is still time to fix them, not on the day
Carry forward what is unfinishedAn incomplete action is tracked, not silently dropped
❞ Expert Insight
"The follow-up is where most of it is won or lost. Anyone can have a good meeting. The teams that actually improve are the ones that go back to the list a week later and make sure the things people agreed to are actually getting done."
[Training Program] · 40 Years in the Room
✓ Best Practice
Put the assigned actions somewhere they will be seen again before the next event, not buried in meeting notes. The list that resurfaces at the right time gets done; the one filed and forgotten gets rediscovered only when the same problem repeats. Tie the follow-up to the next planning session so the loop closes itself.
MODULE J · CLOSING THE LOOP
J.10 · The Checklist

The Evaluation Checklist

The whole section reduces to a checklist run after every meeting, presentation, and event. It is the same discipline as the fee defense checklist in Section H, the compliance checklist in Section I, and the file discipline checklist in Section K, applied to learning from experience.

The evaluation is a habit, and habits hold when reduced to a checklist run every time. Run it after every significant meeting, presentation, or event, and the team compounds its judgment instead of repeating its mistakes.

Run the Four Quadrants
  • Strengths named specifically enough to repeat on purpose
  • Weaknesses framed as process problems, not blame
  • Opportunities identified, including objections reframed as openings
  • Threats listed from last time's surprises and current unknowns
  • Every quadrant produces at least one action
Close the Loop
  • Each action has a named owner and a date
  • The evaluation is captured in writing, not just discussed
  • Actions are placed where they resurface before the next event
  • Progress is checked, and unfinished items carried forward
  • The analysis feeds the next planning session at the front, not the file
▦ Rule of the House
If the evaluation produced no weaknesses and no assigned actions, it was not honest and it did not happen. A debrief that only confirms how well everything went has skipped its entire purpose. Run the full grid, name the hard things, and assign the changes.
❞ Expert Insight
"The point of looking back is to be better next time. Strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, threats: say them honestly, write them down, give each one a name and a next step. That is how a team that has done a thing once does it twice as well."
[Training Program] · The Winning Brief

Threats Are Unconfirmed Items
The unsigned contract, the unreplied registrant, the untasked volunteer. Confirmation neutralizes most threats.
Shape of the Evaluation
Someone leads it, all four quadrants covered, captured in writing, ends with assigned actions.
The Golf Tournament SWOT
Real example: early sign-up worked; carts ran short; raise price, add videos; no-shows and logistics were threats.
Each Quadrant Becomes a Move
Keep strengths, fix weaknesses, pursue opportunities, defend against threats. Every box produces an action.
Follow-Up
Confirm owners, set dates, check progress before the next event. Where improvement is won or lost.
The Honesty Test
No weaknesses and no assigned actions = the evaluation was not honest and did not happen.
