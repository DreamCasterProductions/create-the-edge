B.1 · Why Language Matters

Why Industry Language Matters

In professional consulting, the words you use are not decoration. They are instruments of credibility. Every term you choose signals whether you belong in the room or whether you are guessing your way through a conversation that costs your client millions of dollars.

At Create The Edge, vocabulary is a core professional skill ranked alongside proposal writing, fee calculation, and client communication. The language you use in emails, phone calls, proposals, and presentations determines whether clients perceive you as a trusted authority or a generic vendor reading from a script.

This module establishes the foundational terminology you will encounter daily. Every term defined here appears repeatedly in the [Department Manager] training notes, in client-facing documents, in the review process, and in internal communications. Misusing any of these terms is not a minor mistake: it signals to the client that you do not understand the work you are proposing to do.

❞ Expert Insight
"If you say 'blueprints' instead of 'construction documents,' the architect knows you are not from this world. If you confuse an RFP with an RFQ, the client wonders who trained you. Language is the first filter. Pass it or you never get to show what you actually know."

B.2 · Proposal & Document Terms

Proposal & Document Terms

These are the terms you will use most frequently. Every document that leaves Create The Edge uses these terms precisely.
Figure B.2 · Proposal & Document Terminology Reference
TermDefinitionWhy It Matters
RFPRequest for Proposal. A formal document requesting a detailed proposal including scope, methodology, and fee. The response is a decision tool.The majority of Create The Edge work originates through RFPs.
RFQRequest for Qualifications. Asks firms to demonstrate competency: resumes, past projects, credentials. No fee is quoted initially.RFQs take ~4 hours each. Completing one is a competitive advantage since many competitors will not finish.
Consultation AgreementThe legal contract portion of a proposal. Defines scope, fee, phases, payment terms, and exclusions.Every word has legal weight. Must match the cover letter exactly.
Cover LetterThe narrative portion of a proposal. Introduces project context, establishes authority, describes approach, and summarizes scope.The first two paragraphs carry the most weight. No filler, no "we are pleased to present."
Reference LineThe line at the top of the proposal identifying the project by name, client, and project number. Copied verbatim from the client's RFP.Must be identical each time it appears. Inconsistency signals carelessness.
SF330Standard Form 330. A federal government form for architect and engineering qualifications submissions.Narrative sections can be populated from existing proposals.
Scope of WorkDetailed description of all deliverables organized by design phase (SD, DD, CD, BN, CA).What is not in the scope is not Create The Edge's responsibility. Precision protects the firm.
Independence StatementRequired paragraph affirming vendor-neutral status. Confirm the correct version for your engagement context.Not optional. Wrong version signals the reviewer you did not read the RFP.
Pre-Flight Checklist14-point quality assurance checklist applied before the review process.No proposal moves to [Department Manager] without completing it.
⚠ Watch Out
RFP and RFQ are not interchangeable. Calling an RFQ an RFP in your response signals that you did not read the request. Architects sometimes mislabel documents. Your job is to read the content and respond to what is actually being asked, regardless of the label.

B.4 · Project & Engagement Terms

Project & Engagement Terms

You are writing proposals about your client's project. These are the terms that describe the engagement environment — the vocabulary that separates a consultant who understands the work from one who does not.
Figure B.7 · Project & Engagement Terminology
TermDefinitionProposal Context
Scope of WorkThe defined set of deliverables a consultant agrees to produce within a specific engagement. Determines the fee, the timeline, and the boundaries of professional responsibility.The most important document in any engagement. Vague scope = scope creep. Precise scope = defensible fee.
DeliverableA specific, tangible output produced by the consultant as part of the engagement. Must be named, dated, and trackable.Every proposal must list deliverables explicitly. "Consulting services" is not a deliverable. "Phase 1 Programming Report" is.
Change OrderA formal amendment to the original scope that adds, removes, or modifies deliverables, usually accompanied by a fee adjustment.Change orders are not problems. They are the mechanism that protects the consultant when scope expands. Failure to issue them is where margins disappear.
RetainerA recurring fee paid by the client to maintain access to the consultant's expertise on an ongoing basis. Not project-based.The highest-value client relationship structure. Retainer clients pay for access to judgment, not just deliverables.
Value EngineeringThe process of reviewing a scope or specification to achieve the same functional result at lower cost without compromising quality or compliance.A tool for protecting the client's budget without reducing the scope of the engagement. Never suggest value engineering on your own fee.
Professional LiabilityThe legal exposure a consultant carries for errors, omissions, or negligent advice in the performance of professional services.Every engagement creates professional liability exposure. This is why professional liability insurance is non-negotiable and why scope precision is a legal instrument, not a bureaucratic formality.
ContingencyA budget reserve, typically 5–15% of project cost, set aside for unforeseen conditions or scope additions not identifiable at the time of proposal.Propose contingency explicitly. Clients who understand contingency are better clients. Clients who resist contingency are telling you something about how they handle surprises.
Schematic PhaseThe early phase of a design or planning engagement when concepts are developed and evaluated before detailed documentation begins.The most important phase for client alignment. Scope approved at schematic is scope that should not need to change downstream. Rushing this phase is the most common cause of project overruns.
☰ From the Files
The Scrubber vs. Standard Hood. A standard exhaust hood vents grease-laden air outside the building. A "scrubber" system filters and cleans the air before releasing it, allowing ventilation to be routed back into the dining space without depositing grease on guests. This is critical in high-rise rooftop dining, open-concept restaurants, and outdoor seating areas adjacent to cooking. Knowing this distinction demonstrates technical depth in client conversations.

B.7 · Code & Compliance Terminology

Code & Compliance Terminology

Code compliance is not optional. Every Create The Edge proposal addresses code requirements. You need these terms to write accurately about what the project requires.
Figure B.10 · Code & Compliance Terms
TermDefinitionRelevance
AHJAuthority Having Jurisdiction. The local official who approves or rejects building plans and equipment installations.Varies by location. Create The Edge designs to the most restrictive standard.
CFMCubic Feet per Minute. Measurement of airflow volume for sizing exhaust hoods and ventilation.Underestimating CFM = code violation. Overestimating = wasted money.
UL 710Underwriters Laboratories safety standard for commercial cooking ventilation equipment.Ventless equipment must have UL 710 certification to validate the claim.
MasterSpecSpecification writing system producing bid-ready procurement documents without referencing specific manufacturers.Produced during CD phase. Ensures vendor-neutral, competitive bidding.
MEPMechanical, Electrical, Plumbing. The three engineering disciplines providing building infrastructure.Every piece of equipment needs specific MEP connections. Coordination happens in DD and CD.
RFIRequest for Information. Formal question during construction when a conflict or ambiguity is found.Reducing RFIs is a measure of design quality. Thorough DD and CD phases minimize them.
⚠ Watch Out
Create The Edge does not provide code compliance certification. The firm designs to code and coordinates with the AHJ, but does not stamp drawings, provide PE certifications, or issue building permits. Never promise code approval in a proposal; promise code-compliant design.

B.9 · Using Language With Clients

How to Use Industry Language With Clients

Knowing the correct terminology is only half the skill. The other half is knowing when to use it, when to translate it, and how to calibrate to the person across the table.
Figure B.13 · Language Calibration by Audience
Architects & Engineers
Full Technical Language
"We will coordinate MEP loads during DD and produce MasterSpec procurement documents in CD."
Precision is respect. They expect the vocabulary.
Facility Directors & Operators
Functional Language
"We will produce detailed equipment plans showing every piece, where it plugs in, and how much power."
Explain what, not what it is called.
Executives & Board Members
Strategic Language
"Our process gives you budget certainty before construction. You review and approve at each step."
Outcomes, budgets, timelines.
✓ Best Practice
Match the room, then elevate. Mirror the client's language level first. If they say "kitchen plans," you say "kitchen plans." Once rapport is established, introduce the precise term naturally: "When we produce the kitchen plans during Conceptual Design, we call that the equipment layout." This teaches the client your language without talking down to them.
❞ The Advocate Principle
"Your job is not to present options and let the client choose. Your job is to have already evaluated every option, identified the correct one for their situation, and walked them toward it with enough context that they arrive at the recommendation with confidence. That is what expertise looks like from the client's side of the table."
